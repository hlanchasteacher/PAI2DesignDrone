The big idea

Design is a cycle

A solution is not “finished” just because you made it.

  • A problem creates a need.
  • Research helps you understand the people, context and constraints.
  • Ideas become plans, plans become prototypes, and prototypes create evidence.
  • Evaluation sends you back around the cycle to improve.

A
Inquiring and analysing

Be a detective before becoming a maker.

WHO?  ·  WHAT?  ·  WHY?

  • Explain and justify the need: Who has the problem? Why does it matter?
  • Identify and prioritise research: What do you need to find out first?
  • Analyse existing products: What already works? What could be improved?
  • Write a design brief: one clear statement of the problem and intended user.

IDEAS + EVIDENCE

  • Develop several ideas: quantity first; judgement later.
  • Annotate sketches: materials, dimensions, functions and user actions.
  • Present and justify the chosen design using the specification.

In stage B you create a design specification. What makes a specification strong?

A specification must be measurable, so you can later test whether the solution met it. “Must hold at least 8 kg” can be tested — “nice” cannot.
C
Creating the solution

A good plan makes making safer, clearer and more efficient.

PLAN → MAKE

  • Develop planning drawings or diagrams.
  • Construct a logical plan: steps, order, time, tools, materials and risks.
  • Demonstrate technical skills safely and accurately.
  • Follow the plan — but record and justify sensible changes.

Evaluating

Evaluation is evidence-based judgement, not “I like it”.

TEST + FEEDBACK

  • Design testing methods that match your success criteria.
  • Collect results: measurements, observations and user feedback.
  • Evaluate success: which criteria were met, partly met or missed?
  • Explain how the solution could be improved and what you would change next.

Computational thinking

It is not just coding

It is a toolkit for solving problems so that a person or a computer can follow the solution.

  • Formulate the problem clearly.
  • Break it into manageable parts.
  • Spot similarities and reusable solutions.
  • Ignore irrelevant detail while keeping what matters.
  • Create, test, debug and improve a step-by-step method.

Computational thinking · Move 1

Decomposition

Break one complex problem into smaller sub-problems.

Big problems are hard to solve all at once. Decomposition means splitting the problem into parts that are small enough to handle one at a time — and then solving each part.

  • Ask yourself: What are the separate parts of this problem?
  • Example: “A healthier school break” becomes user needs, nutrition, cost, materials, storage, production and testing.
  • In the Design Cycle: most useful in stage A, when you investigate the problem.

Computational thinking · Move 2

Pattern recognition

Identify similarities, repetitions or trends.

When you spot a pattern, you stop solving the same problem again and again. You can reuse what already works and predict what will happen next.

  • Ask yourself: Have I seen something like this before? What repeats?
  • Example: Similar containers, repeated user preferences and common portion sizes in the snack project.
  • In the Design Cycle: most useful in stage A and stage B, when you research and develop ideas.

Computational thinking · Move 3

Abstraction

Focus on essential information and leave out distracting detail.

Abstraction is choosing what matters and ignoring what does not. A model, a map or a diagram is an abstraction — it keeps only the useful information.

  • Ask yourself: What is essential here? What can I safely leave out?
  • Example: Model only the important variables — cost, capacity, usability and waste.
  • In the Design Cycle: most useful in stage B, when you turn research into requirements.
Check your understanding Attempts: 0

You are modelling a delivery drone. Which is the best example of abstraction?

Abstraction keeps what matters for the decision and removes distracting detail. A model with only cost, capacity, usability and waste is easier to reason about.

Computational thinking · Move 4

Algorithmic thinking

Write precise steps that produce a predictable result.

An algorithm is a step-by-step method. If another person — or a machine — cannot follow your steps, the algorithm needs debugging.

  • Sequence: actions happen in a specific order.
  • Selection: choose what to do based on a condition — IF… THEN… ELSE…
  • Iteration: repeat steps until a condition is met.
  • In the Design Cycle: most useful in stage C, when you plan and build.
Check your understanding Attempts: 0

Which instruction is precise enough for a machine to follow?

A machine needs exact conditions and exact actions. The IF… THEN… ELSE… instruction tells the drone precisely what to check and what to do in each case.
Example

A healthier school break

Let’s apply both frameworks to one realistic challenge: SNACK + WASTE.

  • Problem: students want a quick, enjoyable snack with less packaging waste.
  • Decompose: user needs; nutrition; cost; materials; storage; production; testing.
  • Patterns: similar containers, repeated user preferences, common portion sizes.
  • Abstract: model the important variables — cost, capacity, usability and waste.
  • Algorithm: research → choose criteria → prototype → test → improve.

Debugging and iteration

Errors are information about the design

Not evidence that you are bad at designing.

  • Test with realistic inputs, users and conditions.
  • Find the point where the result differs from what you expected.
  • Change one important feature at a time when possible.
  • Test again and document what the evidence shows.
ERROR → CLUE → FIX
How the ideas fit together

Design Cycle + Computational Thinking

The Design Cycle tells you what kind of design work to do; computational thinking helps you do it clearly.

  • Inquiring: formulate the problem, decompose it and research patterns.
  • Developing: abstract the requirements and write success criteria or algorithms.
  • Creating: use logical plans, sequences, decisions and repetition.
  • Evaluating: test, debug, compare evidence and iterate.
